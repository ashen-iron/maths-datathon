The study · Volume II

Not how much, but how fast

Volume I asked how many runs these players score. Volume II asks a distinct question about scoring pace: Strike_Rate, the runs scored per 100 balls faced, ranging from 88.4 to 146.8 in this sample.

Pre-specified benchmark

Assumed target, clearly labelled: μ₀ = 120 runs per 100 balls, which is 1.2 runs per ball or 7.2 runs per over, a conventional par for limited-overs batting. It was written into analysis_ps2.py before the data were loaded. It is an assumption, not a documented league figure, because the competition is not named.

Limitations of the variable

Balls faced, the reference period and the competition are not recorded. Bowlers’ strike rates may rest on few balls. All 30 players are included, so “the mean” refers to a squad with this 10/10/10 role mix.

Task 1 · Random variables & binomial

Par or better: a coin it isn’t

S is the strike rate of a randomly chosen player (continuous, S ≥ 0). B = 1{S ≥ 120} is Bernoulli(p). X = ΣB over n = 30 players counts the par-or-better players, with values 0–30. Could X be Binomial(30, p)?

The three assumptions

Fixed trials: met, since n = 30 players each give one 0/1 outcome. Independence: uncertain, because sampling is undocumented and teammates share pitches and opposition. Constant p: violated, with 10/10, 7/10 and 1/10 by role. Under a constant p = 0.60, a group of 10 would land in the range 3–9.

The design matters

Because the sample fixes 10 players per role, X is a sum of three binomials with variance 3.00, not 7.20. A single binomial overstates the spread 2.4-fold. If players were drawn at random from the whole population, each would share the same marginal p and X would be binomial again. The fixed quotas break the model; the heterogeneity alone does not.

Reflection questions
1Why is the selected quantity appropriately represented as a random variable?

Before a player is selected, their strike rate is unknown. It varies between players and across seasons, and it assigns a number to each possible outcome of the selection, which is exactly what a random variable is. Treating S as random lets us describe its distribution, quantify uncertainty about its mean and test claims about it. B and X are random for the same reason: whether a selected player strikes at par is not known in advance.

2Which assumptions are needed for a binomial model, and how plausible are they here?

The model needs a fixed n (met), two outcomes per trial (met by construction), independent trials (uncertain) and a constant p (violated: 10/7/1 out of 10, with probabilities of 0.0060 and 0.0017 for the extreme counts under a common p). The binomial model therefore does not fit X as sampled. It would be reasonable within one role, or for a simple random sample from the whole population.

MLE needs a full model and makes the observed data most probable. It is consistent, asymptotically efficient and invariant. Unbiased estimators target E[θ] = θ, and robust ones trade efficiency for resistance to outliers. The role-specific MLEs (batters 135.1, all-rounders 124.7, bowlers 106.0) show that the “identically distributed” assumption is strained.

Reflection questions
1What does the likelihood represent when the observations are fixed?

It becomes a function of the parameters. For each candidate (μ, σ²) it measures how probable, or more strictly how dense, the data actually observed would be. It is not a probability distribution over the parameters and need not integrate to 1. It ranks parameter values by how well they explain the fixed data. The explorer above shows this directly: moving away from the MLE lowers the log-likelihood.

2Which assumptions are important for normal maximum likelihood estimation?

Independence, so the likelihood factorises into a product. Identical distribution, so one μ and σ² apply to everyone; this is weak here because of the role differences. A normal form, supported by Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.339. A correctly specified model, because with a mixture the MLE estimates the closest normal, not the truth. And an n large enough for the asymptotic properties to hold; at n = 30, σ² is visibly biased downward by about −7.48.

Reflection questions
1How should a 95% confidence interval be interpreted over repeated sampling?

If we repeatedly drew random samples of 30 and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean. The lab shows this happening. The mean is fixed and the intervals vary. A single computed interval either contains μ or it does not, so we say we are 95% confident, not that μ has a 95% probability of lying inside it. The guarantee also relies on random sampling.

2How do confidence level, sample size and variability affect margin of error?

ME = t* × s/√n. A higher confidence level raises t* (at n = 30: 4.65 at 90%, 5.59 at 95%, 7.54 at 99%). A larger n shrinks the SE like 1/√n (at 95%: 10.71 at n = 10, 2.71 at n = 120). More variability widens the interval proportionally. Studying one role, where the within-role SD is about 8.89 against 14.98, would narrow it sharply.

There is no evidence that the mean strike rate differs from par. This is not proof that it equals 120: the 95% CI (116.34–127.53) is also consistent with means a few points either side.
Significance versus importance

The gap is 1.93 points: 0.0193 runs per ball, or about 0.12 runs per over. Cohen’s d = 0.129. Even if a far larger sample made it “significant”, a difference this size would be practically irrelevant.

Why fix the benchmark first

Chosen after seeing x = 121.93, a benchmark could be steered: 110 would guarantee significance and 122 would guarantee its absence. Either way the 5% error rate would no longer hold. That is HARKing. Writing μ₀ into the code before loading the data keeps the test honest.

Reflection questions
1What does rejecting or failing to reject the null hypothesis imply?

Rejecting would mean the data are unlikely if μ = 120, so we would conclude a difference, with a 5% Type I risk and without saying anything about cause or size. Failing to reject, as here (p = 0.485), means the data are compatible with 120, not that μ equals 120. With power of only 0.424 against a 5-point departure, a real moderate difference could go unseen. The interval 116.34–127.53 shows what remains plausible.

2Why should the benchmark be specified before examining the result?

A p-value is only valid if the hypothesis is independent of the data. A benchmark picked after seeing x can be tuned to produce or avoid significance, which inflates the real error rate. The slider above shows how easily t and p move. Fixing μ₀ = 120 from cricket convention, in code, before loading the data, with a two-sided alternative chosen in advance, prevents this.

Task 5 · Errors & power

The power to notice

A Type I error declares the squad off par when it is on par (probability α). A Type II error misses a real departure (probability β). Power, 1 − β, depends on α, n, σ and the size of the true departure.

Reflection questions
1Which decision error could be more consequential in this context?

For a coach or selector, usually the Type II error. A real pace deficit, for example a true mean of 112, would go uncorrected and cost runs and matches, and at n = 30 the power to detect a 5-point gap is only 0.424. A Type I error triggers unnecessary changes to batting plans, which are usually reversible. If the result were used to justify an expensive or irreversible decision, the Type I error would matter more, and a smaller α would be justified.

2How could power be improved without changing the significance level?

Increase n: about 73 players give 80% power at Δ = 5. Reduce σ: measure strike rate over more balls, study a homogeneous group, or stratify by role (σ ≈ 8.89 gives power 0.846). Use a one-sided test, but only if the direction is fixed in advance. Use paired designs, such as the same players before and after a change. Improve measurement quality.

The verdict · Volume II

On par, underpowered

Mean pace is 121.93 runs per 100 balls (95% CI 116.34–127.53), well described by a normal MLE. There is no evidence of a difference from par 120 (p = 0.485), but power is modest, so “no evidence of a difference” should not be read as “equal to par”.

Limitations

Sampling is undocumented and quota-like. The par of 120 is a labelled assumption. Balls faced are not recorded, so strike-rate reliability is unknown. Role mixture strains the constant-p and identical-distribution assumptions. The data are observational, so no causal claims are made.
