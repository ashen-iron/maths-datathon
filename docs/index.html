Why Runs_Scored

It is the most direct measure of output that applies to every role. It is complete (30 of 30), varies widely (70–389) and supports all five tasks, so the whole inquiry follows a single question. Player_Role is the only categorical variable, and its three levels are exactly what one-way ANOVA needs.

The file has 0 missing values and 0 duplicate rows. Player_ID is an identifier and is never used in a calculation.

Limitation · reference period

The period the counts cover (season, tournament or career) is not documented. Runs are almost uncorrelated with matches played (r = −0.07), so they are unlikely to be career totals. We read them as runs in the study’s reference period.

I
Task 1 · Sampling design

Who, exactly, are these thirty?

The observational unit is one player. The sample is 30 players. How they were chosen is not documented, so we do not assume the sample is random or representative.

Target population
Players of the limited-overs competition or squad system the records come from. League, season and country are not documented.
Sampling frame
Unknown. No registry, list or selection rule is provided.
Selection
Undocumented. The exact 10 / 10 / 10 split suggests quotas.
Sample
n = 30 players, PL01–PL30, with complete records.
An exact 10 / 10 / 10 split has probability 0.0270 under random sampling.

Under simple random sampling from a population with equal role shares, a perfectly balanced split happens only about 2.7% of the time. That points to a designed selection. It could be quota sampling (non-probability) or stratified sampling with equal allocation (probability), and only documented randomisation within each role could tell them apart. Without that, we treat the sample as non-probability.

The quantity of interest is the population mean μ = E[Runs_Scored]. Because role matters so much, μ = Σ wrμr depends on the population’s role shares, and the sample fixes each share at one third.

Reflection questions
1How could the sampling process affect representativeness?

Role mix. Equal quotas force equal role weights. If the real population has more bowlers, the sample over-represents high scorers and x overstates μ. Role explains about 70.9% of run variation, and the role means range from 128.2 to 294.2, so the role weights matter a great deal.

Selection bias. Hand-picked regulars or in-form players inflate output. A bigger sample would not fix this, because it is bias, not noise. Coverage and time: players without records cannot appear, and the reference period is unknown. With n = 30, even a good design leaves a standard error of about 14.96 runs.

2What sampling design could improve coverage of the target population?

Start from a complete frame, such as the official season registration list. Draw a stratified random sample by role, with proportional allocation or with Neyman allocation, which gives more places to more variable strata (batter SD 54.94 against all-rounder SD 35.82). Apply design weights if the shares are not proportional. Teams form natural clusters, so a two-stage design (random teams, then random players) is a cheaper option. In every case, document the inclusion probabilities and a common reference period, and increase n.

Reflection questions
1What does MSE capture beyond estimator variance?

Bias², the systematic error. Variance measures spread around the estimator’s own average. MSE measures spread around the truth. An estimator can be very precise and still consistently wrong. Because both errors share one scale, MSE also lets us compare biased and unbiased estimators fairly, which is how σ² beats s². One caution: resampling MSE ignores selection bias from the non-random sampling in Task 1, and that bias would add to the real error.

2How can sample size affect the precision and MSE of an estimator?

Var(x) = σ²/n, so precision grows in proportion to n and the standard error shrinks like 1/√n. The MSE of the mean falls from 1,294.4 at n = 5 to 218.7 at n = 30, and halving the error needs four times the data. Small-sample biases such as −σ²/n also vanish, so both variance estimators are consistent. Design bias does not shrink with n, though. A larger quota sample just estimates the wrong quantity more precisely.

Players versus averages

The distribution of observations describes how individual players differ: centre μ, spread σ ≈ 81.95 runs. The sampling distribution of x describes how the average of n players would vary from sample to sample. It has the same centre, a much smaller spread of σ/√n ≈ 14.96 runs at n = 30, and a shape that tends towards normal.

The Central Limit Theorem requires independent, identically distributed draws with finite variance, and an n that is large enough for the parent’s skewness. Try it with a deliberately skewed parent: the share of high-output players (Runs ≥ 300), which is Bernoulli with p = 0.167.

In the report (seed 20261118), the skewness of the Bernoulli means falls from 1.811 at n = 1 to 0.801 at n = 5 and 0.338 at n = 30, close to the theoretical γ/√n.
Reflection questions
1Which features support or weaken a normal-model assumption?

Support: mean ≈ median, skewness 0.099, no outliers, a straight Q–Q plot (r = 0.994), empirical-rule shares close to normal, Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.857 and χ² p = 0.607.

Weaken: runs are discrete counts bounded at zero, though the model gives a negative total a probability of only 0.0046. There is mild platykurtosis (−0.386) and an empty bin. Above all, the data mix three role groups with very different means, so a single normal curve describes the data but does not explain them. And n = 30 gives normality tests little power.

2Why can sample means be approximately normal when individual observations are not?

A mean adds up many independent contributions, so extremes in opposite directions cancel. Spread shrinks like 1/√n, skewness like γ/√n and excess kurtosis like κ/n. The two-spike 0/1 parent (skewness 1.79) produces means with skewness 0.338 at n = 30, close to the theoretical 0.33. The CLT needs independence and finite variance, not normal data, and more skew calls for a larger n (here np = 5.0 < 10, so a little skew remains).

Reflection questions
1Why is one-way ANOVA appropriate for this research question?

The question compares the means of one numerical outcome across one categorical factor with three independent groups. One overall F test keeps the Type I error rate at α, whereas three t-tests would raise the chance of a false alarm to about 14.3%. The assumptions hold reasonably well, and the balanced design adds robustness.

2What does a significant overall result establish, and what does it not identify?

It establishes that the three population means are not all equal. It does not show which roles differ, how many differ or by how much, because that needs post-hoc tests, which are excluded here. It does not measure practical importance (η² does that), prove that role causes the difference, or guarantee the result carries over beyond the population sampled.

V
Task 5 · Chi-square goodness-of-fit

Counting against a model

The expected distribution was fixed before any counting. Runs were cut into five classes that are equally likely under the fitted normal model, so 6 players are expected in each.

Why this expected distribution? Task 3 raised a specific hypothesis: Runs_Scored is normal. Using maximum-likelihood estimates (μ = 213.60, σ = 80.58), we cut at the model’s 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles (145.8, 193.2, 234.0, 281.4). Equal-probability classes avoid arbitrary bin widths, and every expected count is 6 ≥ 5.

The role counts (10/10/10) were already known from Task 1, so a role-based test would be circular. We report it only as a supplementary check: χ² = 0.000, p = 1.000. The fit is perfect, which again suggests quota selection.

Reflection questions
1How should the expected distribution be justified?

It should come from a source independent of the observed counts, such as a theory or model, known population figures, earlier studies or the study design, and it should be fixed, along with the class boundaries, before counting. Each estimated parameter costs one degree of freedom, and expected counts should be at least 5. Choosing expected values after seeing the data makes the test circular.

2What does the overall goodness-of-fit result tell you?

It says whether the counts as a whole are consistent with the model. Here χ² = 1.000 (p = 0.607), so the deviations, the largest being 8 against 6 in class 2, are within chance. It does not prove normality, because power is low and binning loses information. It does not locate a misfit (cell contributions do that), it depends on the chosen classes, and it says nothing about causes.

✦
The verdict

Five lenses, one answer

A player here scores 213.60 runs on average (95% CI 183.0–244.2), with spread that a normal model describes well. Role is the dominant source of variation, so role-specific figures say more than any pooled average.

Limitations

Sampling is undocumented, so standard errors cover random error, not selection bias. n = 30 (10 per role) gives assumption checks little power. The reference period of Runs_Scored and the scales of Fitness_Score and Practice_Hours are undocumented. The data are observational, so batting order and opportunity confound role. Resampling uses the sample as its own population.
