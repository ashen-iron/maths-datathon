Task 1

Random variables & the binomial model

Variables · Strike_Rate → event S ≥ 120 · context Player_Role

1.1Defining the random variables

  • S is the strike rate of a randomly selected player from the target population. It is continuous in practice (100 × runs ÷ balls faced), with possible values S ≥ 0 and no fixed upper limit. Observed values run from {{sr_min}} to {{sr_max}}.
  • B = 1{S ≥ 120} marks whether that player strikes at par or better. It is a Bernoulli variable with values {0, 1} and success probability p = P(S ≥ 120).
  • X = B1 + … + B30 is the number of par-or-better players among n = 30. It is a count with possible values 0, 1, …, 30.

In this sample X = {{X}}, so p = {{p_hat}}. If X ~ Binomial(30, p) with p = {{p_hat}}, then E[X] = 30p = {{binom_mean}}, Var(X) = 30p(1 − p) = {{binom_var}} (SD {{binom_sd}}), the central 95% range is {{binom_lo}}–{{binom_hi}}, and P(X = {{X}}) = {{pmf_obs}}.

1.2Could X be binomial? The three assumptions

Table 1.2Binomial assumptions for X
AssumptionWhat it requiresPlausibility here

IndependenceOne player’s outcome does not change another’sUncertain. Players are distinct, but the sampling is undocumented, and teammates share pitches, opposition and team tactics, which could create positive dependence.

How strong is the evidence that p is not constant? If every player had p = {{p_hat}}, each role of 10 would be expected to show {{role_exp}} successes, with a central 95% range of {{r_lo}}–{{r_hi}}. The probability that all 10 batters reach par would be {{p_bat_all}}, and the probability that at most one bowler does would be {{p_bowl_le}}. Both observed role counts lie outside the likely range, so a single constant p is not credible.

The design matters. Volume I showed that the sample fixes 10 players per role. X is then a sum of three binomials, Bin(10, 1.0) + Bin(10, 0.7) + Bin(10, 0.1), with variance {{quota_var}} (SD {{quota_sd}}) and a central 95% range of {{quota_lo}}–{{quota_hi}}. A single Binomial(30, 0.6) overstates the variance by a factor of {{var_ratio}} (Figure 1A). If players were instead drawn at random from the whole population, each trial would have the same marginal probability p, and X would be binomial again, provided the players were independent. Heterogeneity alone does not break the binomial model; the fixed quotas do.

Reflection questions
1
Why is the selected quantity appropriately represented as a random variable?

Before a player is selected, their strike rate is unknown. It varies from player to player and, for the same player, from season to season, and it attaches a number to each possible outcome of the selection process. That is exactly what a random variable is: a function from uncertain outcomes to numbers, with a probability distribution. Treating S as random lets us describe its distribution (Task 2), quantify uncertainty about its mean (Task 3) and test claims (Task 4). The derived B and X are random for the same reason. Which player is selected, and therefore whether the event occurs, is not known in advance.

2
Which assumptions are needed for a binomial model, and how plausible are they here?

The model needs a fixed number of trials (n = 30: met), two outcomes per trial (par or better, or not: met by construction), independent trials (uncertain, because sampling is undocumented and teammates share conditions) and a constant success probability (violated: {{x_bat}}/10, {{x_ar}}/10 and {{x_bowl}}/10 by role, with probabilities of {{p_bat_all}} and {{p_bowl_le}} for the extreme counts under a constant p). The binomial model is therefore not appropriate for X as sampled. It would be reasonable within a single role, or for a simple random sample from the whole population, where each trial has the same marginal p.

Task 2

Maximum likelihood for a normal model

Variable · Strike_Rate (continuous, n = {{n}})

2.1The model and its assumptions

We model the strike rates as S1, …, Sn ~ iid N(μ, σ²). This assumes (i) independence between players, (ii) identical distribution, meaning every player shares the same μ and σ², (iii) a normal shape: continuous, symmetric and thin-tailed, and (iv) fixed parameters over the reference period.

2.2The likelihood and its maximum

With the data held fixed, the likelihood is a function of the parameters:

L(μ, σ²) = ∏ (2πσ²)−1/2 exp{ −(si − μ)² / (2σ²) }
ℓ(μ, σ²) = log L = −(n/2) log(2π) − (n/2) log σ² − Σ(si − μ)² / (2σ²)

Setting the score equations to zero gives the closed-form maximum:

∂ℓ/∂μ = Σ(si − μ)/σ² = 0  ⇒  μ = s̄      ∂ℓ/∂σ² = −n/(2σ²) + Σ(si − μ)²/(2σ⁴) = 0  ⇒  σ² = (1/n) Σ(si − s̄)²

The second derivatives are negative at this point, so it is a maximum, not a saddle.

MLE chooses the parameter values that make the observed data most probable under a fully specified model. Its estimates are consistent, asymptotically unbiased and efficient, and invariant: the MLE of σ is simply √σ². Unbiased estimators instead require E[θ] = θ, which is why s² divides by n − 1 and is larger than σ² by a factor of n/(n − 1). Method of moments matches sample moments to population moments and needs no likelihood; for the normal model it coincides with the MLE. Robust estimators (median, MAD, IQR) give up efficiency to resist outliers. Their smaller variance estimates show that the bulk of players is tighter than the classical variance suggests, because the bowlers’ low strike rates widen it. Volume I’s resampling showed the same trade-off: the divisor-n estimator, which is the MLE, had lower MSE than the unbiased one.

An assumption under strain

“Identically distributed” is doubtful. The role-specific MLEs are batters μ = {{bat_mu}} (σ = {{bat_sd}}), all-rounders {{ar_mu}} ({{ar_sd}}) and bowlers {{bowl_mu}} ({{bowl_sd}}). The pooled normal describes the squad as a whole, but the data look like a mixture of three groups. A model with role-specific means would be more faithful.

Reflection questions
1
What does the likelihood represent when the observations are fixed?

Once the 30 strike rates are observed, they are no longer random. The likelihood L(μ, σ²) is then a function of the parameters. It scores how probable (strictly, how dense) the data we actually saw would be under each candidate (μ, σ²). It is not a probability distribution over the parameters: it need not integrate to 1, and it does not say how likely a value of μ is. It ranks parameter values by how well they explain the fixed data. Figure 2B shows this as a surface whose highest point is the MLE. Values like μ = 110 sit far down the surface because they would make these data very improbable.

2
Which assumptions are important for normal maximum likelihood estimation?

Independence lets the likelihood factorise into a product. Identical distribution means one μ and one σ² describe everyone; this is the weakest assumption here, given the role differences. The normal form determines the shape of the likelihood; it is supported by Shapiro–Wilk p = {{sw_p}}, though the left tail is mildly heavy. The model must be correctly specified, because if the true distribution is a mixture, the “MLE” estimates the closest normal, not the truth. Finally, a large enough n is needed for the asymptotic properties (efficiency and standard errors from the Fisher information) to be reliable. With n = 30 they are approximate, and σ² is visibly biased downward by about {{bias_s2}}.

Reflection questions
1
How should a 95% confidence interval be interpreted over repeated sampling?

The 95% describes the method, not this particular interval. If we repeatedly drew random samples of 30 and built a t-interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean. Figure 3A shows this: {{cover100}} of 100 intervals did, and {{cov_emp}}% did over {{B}} repetitions. The true mean is fixed and the interval is what varies. Once computed, ({{ci_lo}}, {{ci_hi}}) either contains μ or it does not. It is wrong to say “there is a 95% probability that μ is in this interval”. We say we are 95% confident, meaning the interval came from a procedure that succeeds 95% of the time. The guarantee also assumes random sampling, which this dataset does not document.

2
How do confidence level, sample size and variability affect margin of error?

ME = t* × s/√n, and each factor enters directly. A higher confidence level raises t* and widens the interval: at n = 30, ME is {{me_90_30}} at 90%, {{me_95_30}} at 95% and {{me_99_30}} at 99%. A larger sample shrinks the SE like 1/√n, and also slightly lowers t*: at 95%, ME falls from {{me_95_10}} at n = 10 to {{me_95_30}} at n = 30 and {{me_95_120}} at n = 120, and halving it needs about four times the data. Greater variability raises ME proportionally. Analysing a more homogeneous group, such as one role, where the within-role SD is about {{sigma_w}} against {{s}} overall, would cut the margin of error substantially.

Variable · Strike_Rate · benchmark · μ₀ = {{mu0}} (assumed target, pre-specified)

4.1The claim and the benchmark

Claim tested: the mean strike rate of the population these players represent equals par. Benchmark: an assumed target of μ₀ = {{mu0}} runs per 100 balls (1.2 runs per ball, 7.2 runs per over), a conventional par for limited-overs batting. Statistical significance versus practical importance

The observed difference is x̄ − μ₀ = {{diff}} runs per 100 balls, which is {{diff_rpb}} runs per ball or about {{diff_rpo}} runs per over. Cohen’s d = {{d}} is very small. Even if a much larger sample made this difference “significant”, it would hardly matter on the field. Conversely, the absence of significance here partly reflects low power (Task 5). Practically important departures of 5 points or more would be detected less than half the time at n = 30.

Reflection questions
1
What does rejecting or failing to reject the null hypothesis imply?

Rejecting H₀ would mean the data are unlikely (p < 0.05) if the mean really were 120, so we would conclude the mean differs from par. That conclusion carries a 5% Type I error risk, and it says nothing about cause or practical size. Failing to reject, as here (p = {{p}}), means the data are compatible with a mean of 120. It is not proof that the mean is 120. The test simply lacked evidence of a difference, and with power of only {{power5}} against a 5-point departure, a real moderate difference could easily go undetected. The confidence interval states what remains plausible: anything from {{ci_lo}} to {{ci_hi}}.

2
Why should the benchmark be specified before examining the result?

The p-value is only valid if the hypothesis was fixed independently of the data. If the benchmark were chosen after seeing x̄ = {{xbar}}, it could be steered towards a desired conclusion. Picking 110 would guarantee “significance”, and picking 122 would guarantee its absence. Either way the 5% error rate would no longer hold. This is a form of p-hacking, or HARKing (hypothesising after the results are known). Fixing μ₀ = 120 from cricket convention, writing it into the code before loading the data, and choosing the two-sided alternative in advance keeps the test honest and reproducible.

Type I & Type II errors, and power

Test · the Task 4 one-sample t test (H₀: μ = {{mu0}}, two-sided, α = 0.05, n = {{n}}) · no second inferential procedure

5.1The two decision errors, in context

Table 5.1Decision outcomes for the strike-rate test

Reject H₀Type I error (probability α = 0.05). We conclude the squad scores off par when it does not, which could lead to unnecessary changes to batting plans or a false claim about scoring pace.Correct decision. Its probability is the power, 1 − β.

Here we failed to reject H₀, so the only error we could have made is a Type II error. The design fixes α. β is not fixed: it depends on how far the true mean is from 120. Power is the probability that |T| exceeds the critical value, where T follows a noncentral t distribution with df = n − 1 and noncentrality Δ√n/σ, and Δ = μ − 120.

Checking the error rates by simulation

With {{B}} simulated samples of 30 from a normal population with mean 120, the test rejected {{typeI_norm}} of the time, matching α = 0.05. Using the data’s own skewed shape shifted to mean 120, it rejected {{typeI_emp}} of the time, so the test is robust to the mild skew. At a true mean of 125, simulated power was {{power5_norm}} for normal data, against an exact {{power5}}, and {{power5_emp}} with the empirical shape. We report power for pre-specified departures rather than “observed power”. Observed power is just a re-expression of the p-value and adds no information.

Reflection questions
1
Which decision error could be more consequential in this context?

For a coach or selector, the Type II error is usually the more costly. Missing a genuine shortfall in scoring pace, for example a squad that truly scores at 112, means no corrective action, and in limited-overs cricket a pace deficit of 8 points costs runs and matches. With power of only {{power5}} against a 5-point departure, this risk is real at n = 30. A Type I error would trigger unnecessary changes to batting plans or training. That has a cost, but it is usually reversible. If the claim were used to justify an expensive or irreversible decision, such as dropping players or a public performance claim, the Type I error would matter more, and a smaller α would be justified.

2
How could power be improved without changing the significance level?

(1) Increase n. About {{n80_5_s}} players would give 80% power for a 5-point departure. (2) Reduce variability. Measure strike rate over more balls or a longer reference period. Study a more homogeneous population, such as specialist batters. Or stratify by role and use a stratified estimator: the within-role SD of {{sigma_w}} would lift power at Δ = 5 from {{pw_5_s}} to {{pw_5_w}} at the same n. (3) Use a one-sided test, but only if the direction (for example “below par”) is specified before seeing the data. (4) Use paired or repeated measurements, such as the same players before and after a training change, so between-player variability cancels out. (5) Improve measurement quality, for example by excluding very small numbers of balls faced.

6.1Answer to the research question

The players in this sample score at a mean pace of {{xbar}} runs per 100 balls (95% CI {{ci_lo}}–{{ci_hi}}). A normal model fitted by maximum likelihood describes the spread well (σ = {{sd_mle}}). {{X}} of 30 players reach the par of 120, but that rate depends strongly on role, so a simple binomial model for the count does not hold. Against the pre-specified par of 120 there is no evidence of a difference (t = {{t}}, p = {{p}}), and the observed gap of {{diff}} points is practically negligible. The test is underpowered for moderate departures, however (power {{power5}} at 5 points), so “no evidence of a difference” should not be read as “equal to par”.

6.2Limitations

  • Undocumented, quota-like sampling. Independence and representativeness cannot be assumed, and the CI and test cover random error only.
  • Assumed benchmark. The par of 120 is a labelled assumption, not a documented figure for this competition.
  • Strike-rate reliability. Balls faced are not recorded, so the precision of each player’s rate, especially bowlers’, is unknown.
  • Mixture of roles. The identical-distribution and constant-p assumptions are strained by large role differences.
  • Observational data. No causal claim is made about why some players score faster.
