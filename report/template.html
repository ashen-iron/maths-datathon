0.1Why Runs_Scored is the outcome

Runs_Scored is the most direct measure of output that applies to every role. It is complete (30 of 30 values), it varies a lot ({{min_Runs_Scored}} to {{max_Runs_Scored}}), and it can be summarised, estimated, modelled and grouped. That makes one variable usable for all five tasks, so the report follows one statistical question from start to finish. Player_Role is the only categorical variable and has exactly three independent levels, which is what one-way ANOVA needs.

The period the counts cover (season, tournament or career) is not documented. Runs are almost uncorrelated with matches played (r = {{r_runs_matches}}), so they are unlikely to be career totals. 1.1The documented sampling process

The sampling process is not documented. The file has no sampling frame, selection rule, inclusion probabilities, time window or response information. Following the instructions, we therefore do not assume the sample is random or representative.

One feature of the data is informative. The roles are perfectly balanced at 10 / 10 / 10. Under simple random sampling from a population with equal role shares, the probability of an exact 10/10/10 split is only {{p_balance}} (about {{p_balance_pct}}%, from the multinomial distribution). Real squads also rarely have equal role shares. Exact balance therefore points to a designed selection with a fixed number of players per role. That could be quota sampling (non-probability) or stratified sampling with equal allocation (probability), and only documented randomisation within each role would separate the two. With no such documentation we classify the sample as non-probability (quota-like). The sequential IDs also suggest a list extract rather than a random draw.

1.2Variable and population quantity of interest

The chosen variable is Runs_Scored. The main population quantity is the population mean

μ = E[Runs_Scored]  for the target population of playersParameter

This is the expected run output of a randomly chosen player. We estimate it with the sample mean x (Task 2). Two related quantities appear later: the role-specific means μBat, μAR and μBowl (Task 4), and the population role shares (Task 5). Because role is strongly related to runs (η² = {{eta2}} in Task 4), μ is a weighted average of the role means, μ = Σ wr μr, with weights equal to the population role shares. The sample fixes every weight at 1/3, so x estimates μ without bias only if the population really has equal role shares.

Reflection questions
1
How could the sampling process affect representativeness?

Role mix. A fixed 10/10/10 quota forces equal role weights. If the real population has, say, more bowlers than batters, the sample over-represents high scorers and x overstates μ. If it has fewer, x understates μ. Role explains about {{eta2_pct}}% of run variation, so this is the largest single threat. Role means range from {{bowl_mean}} to {{bat_mean}} runs, so the role weights matter a great deal.

Selection bias within roles. If an analyst or coach chose regular or high-profile players, the sample may favour players with more opportunities and better form. A bigger sample would not remove this, because it is a bias, not random error.

Coverage and time. Players without records (injured, newly registered, rarely selected) cannot appear. The undocumented reference period may also mix seasons. Finally, n = 30 means that even a well-designed sample has a standard error of about {{se}} runs for the mean.

2
What sampling design could improve coverage of the target population?

Start from a complete sampling frame, such as the official registration list for the competition and season. Then use stratified random sampling by role: draw players at random within each role stratum, and set the stratum sizes in proportion to the population role shares (proportional allocation). Alternatively, use Neyman allocation, which assigns more players to more variable strata. The batter SD ({{bat_sd}}) is larger than the all-rounder SD ({{ar_sd}}), so batters would get more places. If the role shares are not proportional in the sample, apply design weights. Teams form natural clusters, so a two-stage design (random teams, then random players within teams) would be cheaper, at some cost in precision. In every case, document the frame, the selection probabilities and a common reference period for Runs_Scored, and increase n where possible.

2.2Point estimation, bias, variance and MSE

A point estimator θ is a rule that turns a sample into a single best guess of a population parameter θ. Its value for our data is the estimate. The estimator is a random variable, because a different sample would give a different value, so we judge it by how its sampling distribution sits around θ:

Bias(θ) = E[θ] − θ   ·   Var(θ) = E[(θ − E[θ])²]   ·   MSE(θ) = E[(θ − θ)²] = Var(θ) + Bias(θ)²

Bias is systematic error, meaning the estimator is off target on average. Variance is random error, meaning how much the estimates spread out. MSE measures total accuracy around the true value. Under independent sampling the theory is exact: E[x] = μ, so x is unbiased, and Var(x) = σ²/n, so MSE(x) = σ²/n. Our estimate of this is {{mse_hat}} runs², an RMSE of {{se}} runs. Likewise E[s²] = σ², so s² is unbiased, while the plug-in σ² = (n−1)s²/n has bias −σ²/n.

Reflection questions
1
What does MSE capture beyond estimator variance?

MSE adds bias², the squared systematic error. Variance measures spread around the estimator’s own average. MSE measures spread around the true parameter. An estimator can be very precise yet consistently wrong, and only MSE would show that. Because it combines both errors on one scale, MSE also lets us compare biased and unbiased estimators fairly. That is how Table 2.3 shows the biased σ² ({{t2_S2n_5_mse}} million runs⁴ at n = 5) beating the unbiased s² ({{t2_S2_5_mse}} million). One caution: the resampling MSE covers only sampling error under random sampling from the pseudo-population. Bias from the non-random selection described in Task 1 would add to the true MSE, and it cannot be estimated from these data.

2
How can sample size affect the precision and MSE of an estimator?

For the mean, Var(x) = σ²/n, so precision grows in proportion to n and the standard error shrinks like 1/√n. In Figure 2B the MSE falls from {{t2_mean_5_mse}} runs² at n = 5 to {{t2_mean_30_mse}} at n = 30. Halving the standard error needs four times as many players, so returns diminish. The bias of σ² (−σ²/n) also vanishes as n grows, so both variance estimators are consistent and their MSEs meet at large n ({{t2_S2_30_mse}} against {{t2_S2n_30_mse}} million at n = 30). A larger sample does not fix design bias, though. A larger quota or convenience sample gives a more precise estimate of the wrong quantity.

3.2Observations versus the sampling distribution of the mean

The distribution of observations describes how individual players differ. It is centred at μ with spread σ (estimated as {{s}} runs), and its shape is whatever shape the data have. The sampling distribution of x describes how the average of n players would vary across repeated samples. It has the same centre μ, but its spread is only σ/√n (about {{se}} runs at n = 30), and its shape tends towards normal as n grows. The first describes players. The second describes our estimate of the mean, and it is what confidence intervals and tests rely on.

3.3The Central Limit Theorem, illustrated

Statement. If X1, …, Xn are independent and identically distributed with mean μ and finite variance σ², then (x − μ)/(σ/√n) approaches N(0, 1) as n → ∞. Conditions: (i) independent observations, for example from random sampling, with n no more than about 10% of a finite population when sampling without replacement; (ii) a common distribution; (iii) finite variance; (iv) n large enough for the parent’s skewness. A rule of thumb is n ≥ 30 for moderate skew, more for strong skew, and np ≥ 10 and n(1−p) ≥ 10 for proportions.

We resampled B = {{B}} samples with replacement from two parents built from the data. The first is Runs_Scored itself. The second is a deliberately skewed indicator of a high-output player, I(Runs ≥ 300). {{high_n}} of 30 players qualify, so this parent is Bernoulli with p = {{high_p}} and skewness {{gamma_high}}. Its sample mean is the proportion of high-output players.

Reflection questions
1
Which features support or weaken a normal-model assumption?

Support: mean ≈ median; skewness {{skew}}; no outliers; a straight Q–Q plot inside the envelope (r = {{qq_r}}); empirical-rule shares of {{w1}}% / {{w2}}% / {{w3}}%; Shapiro–Wilk p = {{sw_p}}; and the χ² goodness-of-fit result in Task 5 (p = {{p5}}).

Weaken: (i) runs are discrete counts bounded below by zero, while the normal model is continuous and unbounded. The fitted model gives a negative total a probability of only {{p_negative}}, so this matters little in practice. (ii) The excess kurtosis of {{kurt}} and an empty bin around 125–150 runs suggest a flatter shape. (iii) Most importantly, the data mix three role groups with very different means (Task 4). The pooled distribution looks normal partly because the three groups are evenly spaced. A single normal curve describes the data well but does not reflect how they arise. (iv) With n = 30, tests of normality have little power.

2
Why can sample means be approximately normal when individual observations are not?

A mean is a sum of many independent contributions divided by n. Unusual values in opposite directions cancel. The spread shrinks like 1/√n, and the shape distortions shrink faster: skewness falls like γ/√n and excess kurtosis like κ/n. Table 3.2 shows this directly. The 0/1 parent, which is as far from normal as a variable can be (two spikes, skewness {{gamma_high}}), produces means with skewness {{clt_h_5_sk}} at n = 5 and {{clt_h_30_sk}} at n = 30, close to the theoretical {{skth_h_30}}. This is the Central Limit Theorem. It needs independence and finite variance, not normal data, and the more skewed the parent, the larger n must be.

Statistical significance versus practical importance

The p-value shows the role differences are very unlikely to be due to chance. The effect size shows they are also large. η² = SSB/SST = {{eta2}}, so {{eta2_pct}}% of the variation in runs is associated with role. The less biased ω² is {{omega2}}, and Cohen’s f = {{cohen_f}} (0.40 already counts as “large”). Descriptively, the role means span {{mean_range}} runs, about {{range_in_sd}} pooled SDs (pooled SD = {{pooled_sd}}). A gap of this size matters for selection and planning. It is observational, though. Roles are not randomly assigned, batters are picked for batting and usually bat higher in the order, so role reflects both skill and opportunity. We do not claim that changing a player’s role would change their runs.

Reflection questions
1
Why is one-way ANOVA appropriate for this research question?

The question compares the means of one numerical outcome across one categorical factor with three independent groups, which is exactly the one-way ANOVA design. Using one overall F test rather than three separate two-sample t-tests keeps the Type I error rate at α. Three independent tests at 5% would carry roughly a {{fwer3_pct}}% chance of at least one false alarm (1 − 0.95³ = {{fwer3}}). The assumptions are reasonably met: independent groups, approximate within-group normality (all Shapiro p > 0.05) and similar variances (Levene p = {{lev_p}}, SD ratio {{sd_ratio}}). The balanced design adds robustness.

2
What does a significant overall result establish, and what does it not identify?

It establishes, at the 5% level, that the three population means are not all equal: at least one role’s mean differs from another. It does not show which roles differ, how many differ, or by how much. Answering that would need post-hoc comparisons such as Tukey’s HSD, which the task excludes. It also does not measure practical importance (for that we used η² and ω²), prove that role causes the difference, or carry over to players outside the population sampled. Given the undocumented, quota-like sampling of Task 1, that generalisation is itself uncertain.

5.1Forming the categories and fixing the expected distribution

The only categorical variable, Player_Role, has counts we already saw in Task 1 (10/10/10). A test whose expected distribution is chosen after seeing the counts would be circular. Our primary test therefore makes a justified category from the numerical Runs_Scored, with an expected distribution fixed by a model before any counting.

Rationale. Task 3 raised a specific hypothesis: Runs_Scored is normally distributed. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test checks that hypothesis in a different way, using counts instead of order statistics. Using the fitted normal model N(μ, σ²) with maximum-likelihood estimates μ = {{mu_mle}} and σ = {{sd_mle}}, we cut the run scale at the model’s 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles ({{cut1}}, {{cut2}}, {{cut3}}, {{cut4}}). This gives K = 5 equal-probability classes, each with expected probability 0.2 and expected count 30 × 0.2 = 6. Equal-probability classes avoid arbitrary bin widths, and K = 5 keeps every expected count comfortably above 5 while leaving positive degrees of freedom after estimating two parameters.

Reflection questions
1
How should the expected distribution be justified?

It must come from a source independent of the observed counts and be stated before the counts are seen. Valid sources are a theory or model (here, the normal model from Task 3), known population figures (for example, role shares from an official registration list, which this dataset does not provide), earlier studies, or the study design. The class boundaries must be fixed in advance too. Every parameter estimated from the data costs one degree of freedom (two here). Expected counts should be at least 5, which our equal-probability construction guarantees (6 per class). Choosing expected values or bins after looking at the data makes the test circular, which is why the role test is reported only as supplementary.

2
What does the overall goodness-of-fit result tell you?

It tells us whether the observed counts, taken together, are consistent with the hypothesised distribution. Here χ² = {{chi2}} (p = {{p5}}), so the deviations, the largest being 8 against 6 in class 2, are well within chance variation. The normal model is not contradicted. It does not prove the data are normal: with n = 30 and five classes, the test has low power, and binning discards information. It also does not locate a misfit when one exists (for that, inspect the cell contributions or standardised residuals), and it depends on the chosen classes. Like every test here, it is about association and fit, not causation.

6.1Answer to the research question

A player in this sample scores {{xbar}} runs on average (95% CI {{ci_lo}}–{{ci_hi}}). Individual run totals are spread widely (SD {{s}}), but they are well described by a normal model. Playing role is the dominant source of variation. Mean runs differ across roles with very strong statistical evidence (F = {{F}}, p = {{pF_sci}}), and role accounts for about {{eta2_pct}}% of the variation. Because the role mix in the sample was fixed rather than random, the overall mean depends on that mix. A population with a different balance of roles would have a different mean, so role-specific summaries are more informative than a single pooled figure.

6.2Limitations

  • Undocumented, quota-like sampling. Representativeness cannot be assumed. Standard errors cover random error only, not selection bias.
  • Small sample. With n = 30 (10 per role), assumption checks have little power and the SE of the mean is about {{se}} runs.
  • Definitions. The reference period of Runs_Scored and the scales of Fitness_Score and Practice_Hours are undocumented.
  • Observational data. Role is not randomly assigned. Batting order, opportunity and selection confound the role–runs association.
  • Resampling scope. The pseudo-population is the sample itself, so resampling cannot reveal what the sample misses.
